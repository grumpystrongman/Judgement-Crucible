# Facilitator Guide

## What you are facilitating

Judgment Crucible is a decision exercise, not a knowledge quiz. The facilitator's job is to preserve tension, force commitment before groupthink takes over, and make the room explain how its reasoning changed.

The strongest sessions do not ask “who was right?” first. They ask:

- What were you protecting?
- What risk did you knowingly spend?
- What assumption made this decision defensible at the time?
- What trigger would have changed your decision?
- Who actually owned the next move?

## Recommended room

- 6–12 participants
- one facilitator laptop
- one large shared display
- participant phones with Wi-Fi or cellular connectivity
- 30–45 minutes for the shipping four-call scenario

For a leadership off-site, put the shared display where everyone can see the countdown without looking away from the group.

## Before participants arrive

- Open `/host` and create a room.
- Put `/room?code=XXXX` on the shared display.
- Confirm the QR code is readable from the back of the room.
- Join once from your own phone as a smoke test, then use a new room for the live session if desired.
- Silence desktop notifications and hide browser chrome if presenting full-screen.

## Opening script — intent, not a score

Explain the ground rules in your own voice:

- Decisions happen before discussion.
- Votes are private; only the aggregate split is revealed.
- A consequence is not the same thing as a grade.
- The Chair must own a tradeoff and a reconsideration trigger.
- Participants should make the decision they would defend, not guess what the scenario author wants.

## Per-call cadence

### 1. Briefing

Let the shared display do the first piece of work. Do not immediately explain the scenario for participants. Ask only clarifying questions that prevent misunderstanding the facts already on screen.

### 2. Private vote

Start/maintain the clock. Discourage side conversations. The value of the reveal depends on participants having committed independently.

### 3. Reveal the split

Do not ask the majority to defend itself first. Give the room a moment to see the disagreement.

Useful question:

> “What assumption would have to be different for the option across the room from yours to become reasonable?”

### 4. Introduce pressure

Advance to the executive-pressure layer. Let the new constraints land before choosing the Chair.

### 5. Chair commitment

Rotate the Chair so the exercise does not become one person's performance. The Chair submits:

- action,
- protected priority,
- accepted tradeoff,
- trigger,
- owner.

Do not rescue the Chair by turning the five selections into a committee discussion. The exercise is deliberately about accountable commitment.

### 6. Consequence

Read the consequence. Then ask the Chair whether the decision remains defensible based on what was known when it was made.

### 7. Carry the debt

Move on before the room resolves every disagreement. Later scenario calls and the Monday After are designed to expose what the room carried forward.

## Debrief

A useful debrief has three levels.

**Decision:** What did we authorize and why?

**Pattern:** What did we repeatedly protect or spend across calls?

**Operating model:** What would need to be true in our real organization for those decisions to be executable?

Avoid turning the debrief into a ranking of participants. The valuable artifact is the reasoning record and the room's repeated pressure pattern.

## Facilitation anti-patterns

- explaining the “lesson” before the Chair commits,
- letting the loudest participant campaign during private voting,
- treating the majority vote as the correct answer,
- over-focusing on technical implementation details that the scenario intentionally abstracts,
- rushing the Monday After reveal,
- using the game as a covert employee assessment without an explicit, reviewed assessment design.
